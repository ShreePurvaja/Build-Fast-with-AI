A1. What the project is about

Most AI assistants are one general-purpose helper. Real businesses do not work that way. A support desk has people who answer questions, people who check orders, people who approve refunds, and a manager who decides who does what.

This platform builds that structure with AI. A business owner says, in text or by voice, "I need to handle customer complaints and refunds." The platform designs a team: a manager and several specialist workers, each with its own instructions, knowledge, tools and permissions. Customers then talk to the team by voice in their own language, including Tamil, Hindi, Telugu and mixed speech like Hinglish. The workers do real things, such as look up an order or start a refund, and pass hard cases to a human with the full story attached. A dashboard shows everything that happened.

The problem: voice bots that only chat cannot finish work, and English-only tools miss most Indian customers. The idea in one sentence: turn a plain-language business need into a managed team of AI workers that speaks Indian languages and completes tasks.

Walkthrough: a Tamil-speaking customer

Setup. Meena runs an online saree store. She types: "Answer customer calls about orders, check delivery, refund if damaged." The platform proposes a Support Workforce: a Manager plus Query, Order Verification and Refund workers, connected to her order sheet and payment gateway. She adjusts the refund limit, runs three simulated calls, and publishes a phone-friendly voice link.

The call. Later, a customer named Kavitha opens the link and says in Tamil that her saree arrived torn. The Manager detects Tamil and replies in Tamil. It routes to the Order Verification Worker, which asks for the order number and looks it up. Kavitha uploads a photo of the damage while still talking. The worker checks the image against the return policy in the knowledge base.

The Manager hands over to the Refund Worker. The refund is under Meena's approval limit, so the worker asks Kavitha to confirm, calls the payment API once, and reads back the result in Tamil. Had the amount been above the limit, the Escalation Worker would have sent Meena's team a summary: transcript, photo, order details and the action already tried.

Afterwards. The Control Center shows the call, the workers used, the refund, its duration, and no failures.

A5. How this product gives more benefit

Gap analysis

No single category above covers all of: generating a team from a requirement, coordinating it in real time, speaking Indian languages naturally, acting in business systems, and escalating with context under one dashboard. Buyers today combine three or four of them.

Where competitors are stronger: voice platforms have mature telephony and latency tuning. Zapier and n8n have far larger integration libraries. Frameworks offer more flexibility for developers. Large vendors have enterprise compliance and support. We should integrate with these rather than fight them on breadth.

Differentiators

Requirement-to-workforce generationA non-technical owner gets a working, editable team from a sentence. This removes the biggest barrier for SMEs: not knowing how to design the flow.
Manager and worker orchestrationNarrow workers with limited permissions are safer and easier to test than one agent with every tool. The manager makes failures traceable to one worker.
Indic-first voiceLanguage detection, code-switching and per-language provider choice are built in, not added on. This is where most global tools are weakest VERIFY.
Escalation with context, plus one control centerHumans receive the full task state, and owners see workers, failures and escalations in one place. This is what makes owners trust AI with real actions.

If a well-funded competitor copies us

A large voice platform could add team generation within a quarter. Ways to stay ahead: (1) build a library of tested Indian industry templates (retail, clinics, logistics, education) that improves with each deployment; (2) go deep on Indian connectors such as UPI-linked payment tools, Tally, Zoho and regional courier APIs; (3) publish per-language quality benchmarks and keep tuning them; (4) offer on-premise and data-residency options that global players prefer to skip; (5) win partners and resellers early.

Recommendation

MVP: hosted SaaS (1) with a web voice link and a simple embed widget (2). Build the control plane as an API from the start so (3) becomes a matter of exposing it. Later, in order: API-first (3), white-label for partners (5), then self-hosted (4) once a regulated customer funds it. The reason: SaaS gives the fastest feedback loop for a small team, and the other models reuse the same core.

Pricing suggestion VERIFY

A free sandbox for testing with capped minutes. A per-workforce monthly platform fee plus usage billed per conversation minute, with a pass-through margin on voice and model costs. Enterprise tier with custom pricing for on-premise and white-label. Validate willingness to pay in five to ten pilots before fixing numbers.

Go-to-market in India

Start with one vertical where calls are repetitive and multilingual, for example D2C e-commerce, clinics, or logistics, and run paid pilots with a handful of SMEs. Sell through agencies and system integrators who already serve them. Lead with demos in the customer's own language, offer WhatsApp and phone-link deployment before app embedding, and price in rupees with simple monthly plans. Use hackathon visibility and case studies for early credibility.

Part B. Architecture

B1. Overview

A stateless control plane (REST API for building, configuring, testing, deploying, dashboards) is separated from a stateful real-time runtime (one session process per live conversation, streaming audio over WebRTC or WebSocket). The control plane writes immutable workforce releases to Postgres. The runtime loads a release at session start and streams traces and usage events back through a queue, so a dashboard outage never breaks a call.

The requirement to workforce flow is: user text or voice, intake, generator LLM, schema validation, editable draft, test run, publish release. The multimodal turn is: image uploaded on a side channel while audio streams, vision model returns a description, the description enters session state as untrusted data, and the manager references it in its next reply. C2. Learning notes per technology

The full seven-point walkthrough for every tool is not expanded on this page. The essentials, in data-flow order: browser mic (WebRTC or WebSocket) then VAD then STT then LLM then TTS then audio back. For each, learn first: Next.js app router, FastAPI async and WebSockets, Pipecat pipelines, Silero VAD, your STT vendor's streaming docs, LLM tool calling and JSON schema output, pgvector similarity search, Postgres row-level security, Redis streams, OpenTelemetry basics. Common beginner mistakes: buffering full audio before STT, ignoring barge-in, forgetting org_id in a query, and logging raw transcripts.

C3. One request through the stack

The caller opens the voice link (Next.js) and gets a short-lived token from FastAPI, which checks auth and the release. The browser streams audio over WebRTC to the runtime. Silero VAD marks speech, streaming STT returns Tamil text, the manager (LLM) picks a worker, the worker calls a tool through the gateway (secrets from the secret store, result in Postgres), retrieval pulls policy text from pgvector, and the answer streams to TTS and back to the caller. Trace and usage events go through Redis to the queue worker, then into Postgres and the Control Center.

D2. Reliability

  • Crash recovery: session state checkpointed in Redis, reconnect resumes at the last turn.
  • Failover: circuit breakers per provider with a secondary STT, LLM and TTS.
  • Retries and dead letters: exponential backoff, then a dead-letter queue visible in the dashboard.
  • Graceful shutdown: stop accepting sessions, let active calls finish or hand off.
  • Idempotency: one key per task step so retries never repeat a refund.
  • Backups: daily database backups with a tested restore, object storage versioning.
